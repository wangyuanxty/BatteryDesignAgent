Rechargeable batteries are pivotal for achieving carbon neutrality and enabling the renewable energy transition. Their advancement requires innovations at micro (materials), device (manufacturing), and system (control and optimization) levels. However, traditional trial-and-error approaches are inadequate for modern scientific demands. As a transformative artificial intelligence (AI) technology, large language models (LLMs) deliver powerful semantic understanding and reasoning capabilities, driving a paradigm shift in battery research to address multilevel innovation needs. Nevertheless, this field still faces dual challenges: ambiguous technical roadmaps and fragmented progress in stage-specific achievements. This review systematically consolidates recent advances in applying LLMs to battery research, distilling core findings across four critical domains: knowledge integration, materials discovery, manufacturing processes, and system management. To address key bottlenecks-including limited model interpretability, inadequate alignment with electrochemical mechanisms, and real-world data adaptation challenges-we propose structured frameworks for deep integration of battery research and LLMs, alongside defined future technical pathways. These frameworks bridge fundamental battery science with AI-driven innovation paradigms to facilitate groundbreaking advances in next-generation battery technologies.

## INTRODUCTION

With the growing global demand for sustainable green energy, rechargeable batteries have become essential components in a wide range of applications, including electric vehicles ${ }^{1}$ and power grids. ${ }^{2}$ According to the Web of Science, battery-related publications have surpassed 1.93 million as of 2024, with a significant surge in research activities especially in the last decade. However, the explosive growth of literature has overwhelmed traditional methods of manual reading and analysis, making it increasingly difficult to stay current with recent advances or systematically retrieve and interpret past research findings. Consequently, a vast amount of valuable scientific knowledge and data remains fragmented and underutilized across the literature landscape. Therefore, the scientific community is in urgent need of tools that can effectively distill and synthesize vast amounts of information into actionable insights.
The development of large language models (LLMs) represents a transformative advancement in artificial intelligence (AI) technology, offering powerful capabilities in semantic understanding, reasoning, and summarization. ${ }^{3-5}$ A major breakthrough came in 2018 with the release of bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT) by Google, which demonstrated impressive contextual comprehension abilities. ${ }^{6}$ This progress was further accelerated by the introduction of ChatGPT by OpenAI in 2022, marking a new era for LLMs. ${ }^{7-10}$ ChatGPT, in particular, exhibits advanced skills in text interpretation and summarization, enabling in-depth analysis of text and efficient extraction of critical insights. The interactive question-and-answer (Q\&A) format of GPT has further lowered the barriers to AI technology, reshaping the paradigm of human-computer collaboration. In early 2025, DeepSeek's open-source architecture reduced the cost and technical barriers of LLM applications, democratizing the accessibility of AI technology in a cost-effective manner. ${ }^{11}$ The rise of the "AI for Science" (AI4S) paradigm has driven a deep integration of AI technologies with scientific discovery. ${ }^{12}$ Leveraging their exceptional knowledge comprehension and logical reasoning capabilities,

Recently, the rapid development of LLMs has brought transformative improvements in this knowledge integration area. Equipped with powerful contextual understanding and reasoning capabilities, LLMs-especially when paired with vectorization techniques-can read and comprehend entire scientific papers much like human experts. Through interactive Q\&A formats, users can extract essential information directly from the scientific corpus. ${ }^{34}$ In addition, LLMs support multilingual processing and natural text generation, enabling communication with researchers across linguistic boundaries. ${ }^{35}$ Their ability to synthesize complex knowledge also facilitates the automated generation of research reports and construction of knowledge graphs. ${ }^{36}$
As shown in Figure 3, the development pipeline for applying LLMs to scientific knowledge integration of battery literature can be mainly divided into three layers: the data layer, model layer, and task layer. ${ }^{34}$ The data layer focuses on collecting battery-related content from diverse sources, including research papers, patents, books, and other technical documents. This stage also involves standardizing and preprocessing data stored in various formats (e.g., PDF, XML, HTML, TXT) and expression types (e.g., text, images, tables, and formulas), ensuring consistency and usability for downstream modeling. In the model layer, the first step is to select an appropriate LLM base model. Then researchers can then choose between three common strategies: (1) fine-tuning the base model on domain-specific data to develop a specialized batteryfocused LLM, (2) leveraging prompt engineering to guide a general LLM without additional training, or (3) building a specialized knowledge vector database and adopting retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) technology enhance domainspecific capabilities of general models while mitigating model hallucinations. This layer also includes model testing and iterative optimization to improve performance and reliability. The task layer involves applying the trained or guided LLM to specific downstream tasks. These may include Q\&A, automated report generation, information extraction, multilingual translation, and the construction of knowledge graphs. Through these applications, LLMs enable efficient knowledge integration and enhance research productivity in the battery domain. Among the three layers in the development pipeline of LLM in knowledge integration, the model layer represents the most critical component. ${ }^{37}$
Regarding the first strategy in the model layer, expert LLMs have demonstrated impressive capabilities in fields such as biology, ${ }^{38}$ chemistry, ${ }^{15}$ and materials science, ${ }^{39,40}$ particularly in tasks like information extraction and domain-specific Q\&A. In the battery field, researchers have recently developed expert LLM models such as BatteryBERT, designed to mine key information on cathode, anode, and electrolyte materials from scientific papers. ${ }^{41}$ Other expert LLMs have also been proposed for extracting manufacturing-related data from broad literature. ${ }^{42}$ However, the development of expert LLMs presents significant challenges. It requires access to large-scale, high-quality, domain-specific annotated corpora, as well as substantial computational resources for model training and fine-tuning. These resource-intensive demands make expert LLM development both complex and costly, potentially limiting their widespread adoption in the near term.
As for the second strategy in the model layer, prompt engineering typically utilizes a multi-turn Q\&A approach, enabling general-purpose LLMs to incrementally build understanding through a sequence of carefully crafted prompts. ${ }^{43-45}$ This strategy enhances precision and efficiency in various tasks, including domain-specific information extraction, expert-level Q\&A, and knowledge graph construction. However, manually designed prompts face three inherent limitations: time-consuming nature, subjectivity, and domain dependency. The design process requires substantial human resources and is susceptible to researchers' cognitive biases; developing prompts for specialized data such as chemical formulas and molecular structures demands deep involvement from domain experts, significantly raising cross-disciplinary application barriers. In contrast, automated prompt generation techniques (e.g., methods based on optimization algorithms and feedback iteration) emerge as promising alternatives through algorithmic approaches: substantially reducing manual intervention to lower trial-and-error costs, replacing subjective judgments with quantitative metrics to enhance prompt consistency and scalability, while leveraging batch-generated domainadaptive prompt templates to overcome specialized knowledge limitations. ${ }^{46,47}$ These automated techniques can improve LLMs' task comprehension capabilities and better unleash their potential. However, it must be emphasized that both manual and automated prompt engineering are ultimately constrained by the fundamental ceiling of the underlying LLM's domain comprehension capability. When models lack specialized knowledge (e.g., in predicting multiscale material behaviors in battery science), even optimal prompt strategies may fail. ${ }^{40}$
Two common issues associated with LLMs are knowledge cutoff and hallucination. Knowledge cutoff refers to the fact that the training data of an LLM does not include content beyond its training cutoff date, meaning that its knowledge is limited to the data available during training. Hallucination, on the other hand, occurs when a user's query exceeds the scope of the LLM's knowledge. In such cases, the model generates responses based on its own understanding, which may be inaccurate. The advent of RAG has addressed these knowledge limitations by integrating external data. ## BATTERY MATERIALS DEVELOPMENT

The development of battery materials requires comprehensive consideration of a wide range of chemical and physical properties, such as redox potentials, chemical stability, crystal structures, mechanical strength, or conductivity. Reliable materials design also depends on detailed data during processes such as material synthesis, characterization, or performance testing. ${ }^{52-58}$ While researchers have curated portions of these data into structured databases, a vast amount remains embedded in unstructured formats within scientific literature and patents. Effectively organizing and structuring this multi-source, heterogeneous data is a crucial step toward advancing battery materials research. ${ }^{59}$ By transforming this unstructured information into structured databases, researchers can significantly improve materials screening efficiency and performance prediction accuracy, thereby accelerating the discovery of novel materials and reducing experimental costs. ${ }^{60}$ Despite these efforts, the pace of innovation in battery materials continues to lag behind the rapidly growing demands of industry. Human-driven approaches alone are no longer sufficient. Deeper integration of AI, particularly LLMs, is essential. In Miret and Krishnan, ${ }^{61}$ the authors outlined a Materials Science LLM that is based on domain knowledge and capable of generating hypotheses followed by hypothesis testing. They also discussed the application of Materials Science LLM to the six interrelated steps of real-world material discovery: material querying, data retrieval, material design, internal evaluation, experimental planning, and experimental execution. This provides new ideas for intelligent, automated new material development based on LLM.
Based on existing research, this paper summarizes a framework for developing battery materials based on LLMs, as shown in Figure 5. LLMs play an integral role in the end-to-end workflow of battery materials development, spanning from information extraction and hypothesis generation to downstream task execution. In Figure 5A, LLMs-guided by prompt-based inputs-extract structured information from diverse data sources such as scientific papers, patents, books, and experimental records. The resulting battery material databases contain rich, multi-dimensional data including material-named entity, chemical composition, microstructure information, physical property, and synthesis method, providing a solid foundation for materials research. Here, the general LLMs can be guided to complete specific tasks through prompt engineering, or an expert LLM can be developed for specific tasks through fine-tuning. Building on this, Figure 5B illustrates the core reasoning process enabled by expert LLMs. Researchers interact with the expert LLM through a multilayered Q\&A process, posing questions related to boundary conditions, key performance indicators, and application scenarios. Based on this input, the expert LLM generates research hypotheses, such as material development program, feasibility analysis, and which experimental equipment is needed. These hypotheses are then passed to a downstream task interface for further processing. In the downstream task phase, as shown in Figure 5C, researchers continue to interact with the expert LLM to perform specific functions such as potential material screening, property prediction, and synthesis path optimization. Ultimately, this extends to automated material preparation, characterization, and testing. Through this iterative and interactive workflow, expert LLMs enable a closed-loop integration of data-driven insights, hypothesis formulation, and experimental execution-offering a transformative approach to accelerate battery materials innovation.

## Battery materials database

A comprehensive materials database is essential for the advancement of battery materials research, as it consolidates large volumes of experimental results, computational data, and scientific literature. By analyzing these data, researchers can uncover correlations between material structures and properties, predict the performance of novel materials, and optimize existing formulations. Such databases also support the design of more efficient experimental protocols, reducing trial-and-error efforts and improving the success rate of experimental validation. However, traditional manual methods for database construction are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and susceptible to human error. These methods often struggle with data consistency and completeness, and are particularly inefficient at processing unstructured data from diverse sources. As a result, the update frequency and practical utility of traditional databases are limited, which in turn constrains research efficiency and the pace of innovation.
In contrast, the application of LLMs can significantly improve the efficiency and scalability of battery materials database construction. LLMs can automatically extract key information from vast quantities of literature and experimental records, integrating and standardizing heterogeneous data to accelerate database generation and maintenance. ${ }^{62}$ For instance, Huang et al. ${ }^{41,63}$ developed BatteryDataExtractor, a literature mining toolkit for battery materials that uses BERT-based models for token classification. ## Developing new materials

In the field of materials development, AI systems based on LLMs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in predicting material properties, forecasting the outcomes of chemical reactions, and autonomously designing, planning, and executing complex experiments. ${ }^{68-71}$ This enables end-to-end intelligent automation across the entire materials development pipeline-from structural design and performance prediction to synthesis optimization, material preparation, characterization, and testing.

For material screening, LLMs enhance efficiency by enabling natural language interaction and integrating with high-throughput experimental techniques to rapidly identify promising material structures or compounds. For example, Pan et al. ${ }^{72}$ introduced a high-throughput screening method for efficient electrocatalysts based on Pt-based high-entropy alloys (HEAs). By utilizing an element library generated by LLMs and combining it with micro-scale precursor printing and pulsed high-temperature synthesis technologies, they fabricated HEA arrays with diverse element compositions. Scanning microscopy was then employed for high-throughput characterization, successfully identifying HEA compositions with high catalytic activity. In another study, Yu et al. ${ }^{73}$ leveraged LLMs along with extensive domain knowledge to perform material screening and evaluation through self-retrieval and self-assessment mechanisms. Jia et al. ${ }^{16}$ proposed a novel material design framework driven by LLM agents, where human instructions are translated into actionable material modifications. This framework supports interpretable and goal-driven material discovery, marking a significant step toward fully autonomous materials research. Robson et al. ${ }^{74}$ proposed a multi-agent network based on gpt-4- 0125-preview that successfully generated aqueous deep eutectic electrolyte compositions for zinc-ion batteries. By analyzing academic papers from relevant fields, the network identified innovative, cost-effective, and sustainable Lewis base candidates. Chen et al. ${ }^{75}$ proposed a chained LLM-human interaction framework that employs GPT-4 for specialized research in chemistry and materials science, focusing on identifying electrolyte materials for the emerging four-electron zinc-iodine (Zn-I2) battery. Through this interactive workflow, GPT-4 retrieves key literature information and recommends promising electrolyte materials for experimental evaluation, thereby elucidating the mechanisms of multielectron zinc-iodine batteries, identifying suitable materials, and enhancing the performance of four-electron Zn-I2 batteries.
LLM-based approaches for predicting material performance are gaining increasing attention. Leveraging structured battery material databases, LLMs can assess the physical and chemical properties of candidate materials. ${ }^{76}$ By integrating LLMs with deep learning algorithms such as graph neural networks, the accuracy of material performance prediction can be enhanced. ${ }^{77}$ Researchers have applied LLM techniques to predict the properties of various battery components, such as cathode materials, anode materials, liquid electrolytes, and solid-state electrolytes. For instance, Lei et al. ${ }^{78}$ constructed a comprehensive knowledge graph encompassing material structures, properties, and research methodologies. By integrating this knowledge graph with GPT-4, they developed a joint reasoning approach for capacity performance prediction of anode and cathode materials for batteries. Soares et al. ${ }^{79}$ introduced a multimodal framework based on the chemical language model MoLFormer, developed to explore the design space of liquid electrolytes. This approach leverages chemical information pre-trained on large unlabeled corpora and demonstrates superior performance in electrolyte Coulombic Efficiencies property prediction.
In synthetic pathway optimization, LLMs can propose innovative reaction routes and conditions. For example, Reinhart and Statt ${ }^{80}$ demonstrated that the Claude 3.5 model outperforms active learning schemes in screening monomer sequences to achieve targeted morphologies in macromolecular self-assembly. Utilizing pre-trained language models significantly reduces the need for hyperparameter tuning while introducing novel capabilities like self-reflection. Lu et al. ${ }^{81}$ integrated LLMs into an evolutionary optimization framework for single- and multi-objective optimization of functional transition metal complexes. The study revealed that LLMs, as generative models, can recommend novel ligands and transition metal complexes with distinctive properties by integrating domain-specific knowledge with external chemical data, thus combining the advantages of efficient optimization and molecular generation.

In material preparation, LLMs are capable of generating detailed experimental protocols and operational steps. When combined with intelligent machinery or robots, they can guide automated synthesis processes. For instance, Ni et al. ${ }^{82}$ implemented natural language interaction with a multi-agent system to enable autonomous control of material preparation, significantly enhancing experimental precision and reproducibility.
LLMs also enhance the efficiency of materials characterization and testing by integrating multimodal data-including electron microscopy techniques (e.g., scanning electron microscopes, ${ }^{83}$ transmission electron microscopes ${ }^{84}$ ) and analytical methods such as spectroscopy (e.g., Raman spectroscopy ${ }^{85}$ ) and diffraction (e.g., X-ray diffraction ${ }^{86}$ ). Leveraging transformer-based multi-head attention mechanisms, LLMs can simultaneously analyze lattice distortions, phase interfaces, and elemental distributions. ${ }^{87-89}$ This enables the construction of quantitative models linking ion transport pathways to macroscopic properties like electrical conductivity, thereby accelerating structure-property relationship analysis. Moreover, LLM-powered code generation is revolutionizing equipment control. The Febba team, for example, used ChatGPT-4 to develop control software for materials characterization equipment, simplifying setup and execution while allowing researchers to focus on data analysis. ${ }^{90}$ Additionally, multimodal LLMs enhanced by vision-language pre-training can recommend relevant literature based on uploaded microscopic images or spectroscopic data. ${ }^{91}$
A prominent example of full-process automation is the A-Lab platform developed by Professor Gerbrand Ceder's team (Figure 6C). ${ }^{65}$ This system uses LLMs to extract synthesis formulas from literature and databases, applies active learning algorithms based on thermodynamic models to refine them, and employs robotic systems to perform synthesis, characterization, and quality evaluation. Compared with traditional manual workflows, this LLM-driven approach improves materials development efficiency by an order of magnitude and significantly reduces R\&D costs. ${ }^{60}$ Zheng et al. ${ }^{66}$ discussed how to customize "chemically aware" models for specific tasks and integrate them into existing mesh-like chemical practices, transforming traditional "make, characterize, use" protocols driven by empirical knowledge into discovery cycles based on identifying synthesis-structure-property-performance relationships. Additionally, they explored integrating modular LLM agents into multi-agent laboratory systems (e.g., autonomous robotic labs) to streamline labor-intensive tasks and collaborate with chemists, as well as how LLMs can reduce barriers to applying generative AI and data-driven workflows to challenging research problems such as crystallization, as shown in Figure 6D.

## BATTERY INTELLIGENT MANUFACTURING

Battery manufacturing research encompasses both battery design and production. Effective battery design requires careful consideration of key performance requirements such as cost, application scenarios, geometric requirements, durability, and energy density. ${ }^{92-94}$ Meanwhile, the cell manufacturing process involves complex steps including slurry mixing, electrode coating-drying-calendaring-slitting-stacking, battery assembly, electrolyte filling, battery formation and aging, each of which significantly influences the final product's performance and reliability. ${ }^{95}$ Currently, optimization of design and manufacturing processes largely depends on trial-and-error experimentation and expert-driven heuristics. These conventional approaches are costly, time-consuming, and often lack the precision needed for high-quality, scalable production. In this context, LLMs offer promising AI-driven strategies to accelerate and enhance battery manufacturing.

## BATTERY SYSTEM MANAGEMENT

Accurate and efficient battery management, such as state estimation and fast-charging strategies, is essential for mitigating battery degradation and enhancing the overall user experience. ${ }^{109,110}$ Traditional approaches to battery health monitoring and safety warnings often rely on manually defined boundary conditions, which can lack adaptability and precision. In contrast, LLMs have recently demonstrated significant potential as innovative tools for intelligent battery management. ## State estimation

In the field of lithium-ion battery state estimation, precise modeling of state of charge (SOC) and state of health (SOH) is crucial for the safe and efficient operation of battery management systems (BMSs). ${ }^{113}$ However, existing mechanistic or machine learning models heavily rely on training data quality, with a key limitation being their overdependence on laboratory-standard databases or accelerated aging data. ${ }^{114}$ While such data provide benchmark information, they fail to capture the nonlinear impacts of real-world complex factors-such as temperature, charge-discharge rates, dynamic operating conditions, and historical cycling stresses-on battery electrochemical behavior and aging trajectories. ${ }^{114}$ Neglecting these environmental variables leads models to learn battery characteristics only under idealized conditions. When deployed in practical scenarios like electric vehicles or energy storage systems, their accuracy, robustness, and generalization capability in SOC/SOH estimation significantly deteriorate, ultimately failing to track batteries' true performance boundaries and degradation paths. ${ }^{115}$

LLM-based battery state estimation technology breaks through this bottleneck with its powerful data-driven capabilities: Its pre-trained models autonomously learn universal deep representations of battery behavior, reducing reliance on manual feature design while mining latent health indicators from massive unlabeled data. The pre-training-fine-tuning paradigm substantially lowers the demand for labeled data, enabling rapid adaptation to diverse battery types, aging states, and environmental stress patterns (e.g., temperature variations/charge-discharge rates) through few-shot transfer learning, thus bridging the gap between laboratory and real-world applications. ${ }^{116}$ The self-attention mechanism of LLMs simultaneously captures instantaneous dynamics during individual charge-discharge cycles and long-term aging trends across cycles, while performing end-toend fusion of multi-source heterogeneous data (time-series electrical signals, environmental parameters, operational condition descriptions). Confronted with inherent defects in real-vehicle data-noise, missing values, and fragmentation-their enhanced fault tolerance and uncertainty quantification capabilities generate more reliable estimations aligned with actual battery performance, providing solid decision-making support for BMSs in dynamic operating environments. Bian et al. ${ }^{117}$ proposed a hybrid prompt learning approach for SOC estimation of lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) using LLMs. A hard prompt generator is first utilized aiming to convert LIB measurements into instruction text for SOC estimation via language modeling. A soft prompt adapter is then constructed to independently encode a small set of parameter vectors to allow the large language model to efficiently adapt to different estimation tasks while maintaining generalization across LIBs. These vectors are optimized and integrated into the LLM as domain knowledge, enhancing the estimation robustness under different temperatures and operations. Notably, under adverse conditions such as low temperatures or high-load operations, LLMs can rapidly adapt and self-regulate using patterns learned from extensive historical data, providing users with timely and precise feedback on battery status. ${ }^{118}$

## Safety warning

Battery failures can lead to performance degradation, operational interruptions, and even serious safety incidents. ${ }^{121}$ Therefore, the early detection of faults is critical for minimizing risk and maintaining system integrity. ${ }^{122}$ The user-friendliness and exceptional natural language processing capabilities of LLMs are recognized for their potential to enhance human-machine interaction efficiency in fault diagnosis. Ma et al. ${ }^{123}$ proposed a novel fault diagnosis pipeline augmented by knowledge graphs. This pipeline employs LLMs to perform complex fault diagnosis tasks while constructing knowledge graphs to improve the models' precision in reasoning. The approach was subsequently evaluated using a proprietary product fault diagnosis database. Experimental results demonstrate that this model outperforms existing RAG models in assisting fault analysis and providing reliable guidance. Compared with traditional threshold-based methods in mechanistic models or fault feature extraction in conventional machine learning algorithms, multimodal LLMs offer a more comprehensive approach. These models can integrate and analyze a wide range of information-including battery sampling data, sensor images, and external environmental conditions-to provide holistic safety assessments. ${ }^{124,125}$ For example, Wang et al. ${ }^{126}$ innovate and validate the temperature excavation (TE) method that interprets the kinetic preferences of thermochemical reactions within minimal experiments into millions of training data. With the help of the TE method, they built the first universally applicable battery thermal runaway model, which achieves high prediction accuracy across a 500°C range on 15 distinct commercial and advanced chemistries with different battery formats and covers all normal working conditions. However, deploying LLM-based safety warning algorithms directly in vehicles poses significant challenges, such as computational overhead and real-time response requirements. To overcome these limitations, Li et al. ${ }^{127}$ developed an LLM agent operating system designed to improve both the performance and efficiency of LLMs in embedded environments. In this system, the LLM functions as the brain, coordinating multiple agents through intelligent resource allocation to enable real-time monitoring and early risk warnings. When deployed on the vehicle side, this agentbased system significantly enhances both response speed and diagnostic accuracy in safety-critical scenarios. Zhang et al. ${ }^{128}$ integrated an LLM with an optimized wavelet packet transform (WPT) to enhance the accuracy of synchronous capacitor fault diagnosis in ultra-high voltage direct current transmission systems. This framework innovatively employs the LLM to automatically optimize WPT parameters, overcoming the limitations of traditional manual parameter selection methods. Compared with conventional approaches, the system maintains high real-time processing efficiency while significantly reducing the false alarm rate. While LLM-based approaches to battery safety warnings show great promise, they remain in an early stage of exploration. Further research is needed to develop knowledge distillation techniques for lightweight LLM deployment and to address challenges related to real-time, onvehicle implementation of these intelligent safety systems.

## Charging optimization

Optimizing fast-charging strategies is a key challenge in improving the charging efficiency and user experience of electric vehicles. This optimization involves multiple factors, including charging speed, temperature regulation, and battery lifespan. ${ }^{129}$ To balance user experience with battery lifespan, it is essential to analyze user preferences on their habits, alongside considering the actual state of the battery when formulating a fast-charging strategy.
Leveraging LLM technology, integrating data such as electric vehicle charging prices and regional electricity pricing strategies can reveal how pricing influences user charging decisions. ${ }^{130}$ For example, by analyzing the online behaviors and social media comments of Chinese consumers, it was found that direct current fast charging is the most popular method, especially in first-tier cities. ${ }^{131,132}$ Concerns about finding public charging stations and range anxiety are identified as key issues from the user perspective, underscoring the importance of fast-charging capabilities. Therefore, LLMs can rapidly collect and organize user charging preferences, generating analytical reports that guide the development of fast-charging protocols and the selection of charging equipment. For instance, Namikoshi et al. ${ }^{133}$ combined LLMs with a multi-armed bandit algorithm to influence consumer preferences for charging methods through personalized dialogue interventions.
In addition to understanding user behavior, it is crucial to develop fastcharging strategies tailored to specific application scenarios using Al. Traditional fast-charging technologies primarily controlled the charging process by setting fixed power levels and charging times to ensure battery safety and efficiency, while lacking robustness to different battery states, environments, or using habits. ${ }^{134}$ Zhao et al. ${ }^{22}$ demonstrated the use of LLMs to construct a fastcharging database that enables rapid, accurate information retrieval through user interactions, as shown in Figure 10D. This approach enhances both research efficiency and innovation in fast-charging strategies. Feng et al. ${ }^{135}$ simulates electric vehicle charging behavior by integrating user preferences, psychological traits, and environmental factors to optimize charging decisions.
Beyond power battery state estimation in electric vehicles, LLMs are increasingly deployed in energy storage systems. ${ }^{18}$ For energy storage optimization, Gao et al. ${ }^{136}$ demonstrate that the DeepSeek model combines multimodal pre-training with reinforcement learning to establish a spatiotemporal multiscale intelligent decision system, enhancing the economic efficiency and operational stability of storage stations. Its safety management employs a crossmodal data fusion early-warning mechanism enabling proactive defense across microscopic defects to macroscopic failure pathways. Simultaneously, DeepSeek leverages semantic associations and data-driven modeling to provide quantitative analytical tools for industry chain assessment and strategic planning. This "system symbiosis and energy-efficiency coevolution" paradigm accelerates next-generation energy storage R\&D, advancing the development of high-resilience, zero-carbon power systems.
Currently, the application of LLMs in battery intelligent management remains limited. One major challenge is that battery management relies on time-series data, while LLMs, which are typically trained on textual data, do not perform well with time-series information. To advance the use of LLMs in battery intelligent management, it is crucial to develop time-series models, such as TimeGPT, ${ }^{137}$ capable of processing this type of data. Building upon state estimation using temporal large models, it is also necessary to integrate battery text information to achieve state estimation based on multimodal data fusion.

## CHALLENGES AND OUTLOOKS Challenges

LLM technology has shown promising potential across multiple areas of battery research-including knowledge integration, materials discovery, manufacturing, and intelligent system management. However, significant challenges remain that currently limit its widespread application and real-world deployment.

In the field of battery knowledge integration, LLMs often exhibit limited understanding of highly specialized terminology and domain-specific concepts. This can lead to misinterpretations or inaccuracies in knowledge extraction. While LLMs have demonstrated strong performance in processing textual content from scientific literature, their ability to interpret non-textual data-such as tables, figures, and graphs-remains underdeveloped. This limitation hinders comprehensive information extraction
from full research articles, where critical data are frequently presented in non-text formats.

In terms of battery materials development, LLMs may struggle to accurately comprehend and apply complex physical and chemical principles-particularly at the microscopic or quantum scale. This limitation can lead to generated recommendations or hypotheses that are misaligned with fundamental scientific laws. Additionally, the reliability of LLM-generated content remains largely unverified and requires rigorous experimental validation. Large-scale simulations and data processing tasks involving LLMs demand substantial computational power, posing a barrier for many research institutions with constrained hardware and budgetary resources.

In the domain of battery manufacturing, LLMs can efficiently summarize technical documents, patents, and production guidelines, while also offering insights into process parameters and manufacturing optimization. However, current research still lacks sufficient empirical evidence to validate the feasibility and accuracy of LLM-based process optimization and quality control in industrial environments. Moreover, much of the critical data in battery production is proprietary or confidential, making it difficult to construct comprehensive, highquality training datasets. As a result, the outputs generated by LLMs may be incomplete or insufficiently precise for practical manufacturing applications.

In battery intelligent management, LLMs face challenges in modeling and interpreting the complex, dynamic electrochemical behaviors of batteries, especially under extreme operating conditions. Their capacity to accurately explain mechanisms such as battery aging or thermal runaway is still limited. Additionally, the development and deployment of high-performing LLMs for battery management require significant investments in hardware infrastructure, data storage, and computational resources-posing further barriers for practical application.

## Outlooks

Despite current challenges, LLMs hold tremendous potential to reshape the future of battery research. As their capabilities continue to evolve, LLMs are expected to play an increasingly central role in intelligent, efficient, and scalable solutions across the entire battery research and development landscape.
A key direction for future advancement lies in strengthening the integration and processing of multimodal data. Battery research spans diverse data types-including text, images, tables, and experimental measurementsrequiring models capable of consolidating and reasoning across heterogeneous sources. The advent of multimodal general-purpose LLMs has opened new avenues for developing specialized LLMs for battery research. By combining textual content such as experimental reports with microscopic structural images and tabular data, LLMs can achieve a more comprehensive understanding of battery knowledge. Using contrastive learning and crossmodal alignment techniques, LLMs can establish connections between various data formats, including text, images, tabular data, and video inputs. This enhances end-toend reasoning capabilities, from electron microscopy feature extraction to analytical interpretation. This integrated approach is a promising pathway to accelerate materials discovery, battery design, and battery performance prediction.
To unlock the full potential of LLMs in battery materials development, it is essential to establish closed-loop feedback mechanisms between experimental validation and model refinement. LLM-generated material design proposals should be verified through laboratory experiments, with the resulting data used to iteratively improve model accuracy. An even more ambitious goal is the development of LLM-centered autonomous systems that combine theoretical knowledge with robotic automation to complete the full research cycle-from material proposal generation and screening to synthesis, characterization, and performance testing. To achieving this vision will require close cooperation between academia and industry: enterprises can provide computational power and infrastructure, while academic researchers contribute theoretical models and experimental insights to create an efficient and intelligent discovery loop.

Embedding first-principles knowledge from physics and chemistry into LLMs is crucial for ensuring scientific validity and reliability. The design and performance prediction of battery materials must adhere to fundamental physical and chemical laws-something general-purpose LLMs do not inherently guarantee. Integrating domain knowledge into LLM architectures will enhance the accuracy and credibility of model outputs. This process requires advanced AI techniques such as symbolic reasoning over physical equations, bidirectional differentiation in neural network representations, and the integration of structured knowledge graphs. Realizing these capabilities will demand deep interdisciplinary collaboration spanning AI, materials science, electrochemistry, and physics.
In the field of battery manufacturing, enabling secure data sharing while protecting privacy is essential. Manufacturing data are often proprietary and sensitive, limiting its availability for model training. Solutions such as data de-identification, encryption, and federated learning can help overcome this barrier, allowing models to be trained on distributed datasets without compromising confidentiality. These approaches will facilitate the construction of high-quality, diverse public datasets for training domain-specific LLMs, thereby expanding the scope and practicality of AI applications in battery production.

For battery intelligent management, improving the quality and structure of time-series data is critical. Time-series data underpin key battery management tasks such as state estimation, fault detection, and life prediction. Techniques such as data cleaning and augmentation can enhance model robustness, while integrating LLMs with physics-informed models can further improve interpretability and accuracy, especially under complex operational scenarios. Developing customized LLMs specifically tailored for time-series data, such as TimeGPT, represents a promising research direction, combining the strengths of statistical modeling and deep learning to address the unique challenges of battery system monitoring and control.
Finally, fostering cross-disciplinary collaboration and open knowledge exchange is fundamental to accelerating progress. Organizing interdisciplinary workshops, seminars, and collaborative projects can bring together experts from AI, battery science, and related fields to tackle shared challenges. Additionally, establishing open-source communities and shared research platforms will promote the dissemination of new findings, facilitate reproducibility, and support the rapid evolution of LLM applications in the battery domain.

In summary, the deep integration of LLMs into battery research holds vast promise. By advancing multimodal data integration, establishing closed-loop feedback mechanisms, embedding scientific principles, promoting data sharing and privacy protection, improving data quality and model robustness, and fostering cross-disciplinary collaboration and knowledge exchange, the full potential of LLMs in the battery field can be unlocked. These developments will drive transformative progress in battery technology and contribute to the realization of more efficient, reliable, and sustainable energy storage solutions for the future.
